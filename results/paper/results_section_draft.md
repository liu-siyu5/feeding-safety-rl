## Experimental protocol

Every condition was trained with PPO for 4 × 10^5 environment steps with three
random seeds, using identical hyperparameters and observation/reward
normalisation. Each trained policy was evaluated deterministically for 100
episodes from randomised start poses. An episode is a **success** if the spoon
reaches the feeding pose — bowl front within 3 cm of the mouth, approach
within 30° of the face axis, bowl tilt within 30°, within 1.5 cm of the mouth's
centre line, and pitched between 20° downward and 2° upward — holds it for
0.48 s, and the contact force never exceeds the danger threshold
F_d = 10 N during the episode. We report the mean ± sample standard deviation
over seeds, with per-seed success rates in Table 1. Besides success we report
the rate of completed holds regardless of force, the peak contact force
(averaged over episodes), the fraction of control steps above F_d, the force
impulse, the fraction of steps in which a shield modified the action
(intervention rate), and the time to success.

## Main comparison

Table 1 and Fig. X summarise the four conditions.

**Unconstrained (C1).** The policy completes the hold in every episode but
never safely: it presses the spoon into the mouth with a peak force of
13.9 ± 0.4 N, and 73 % of all control steps exceed F_d. Its success rate is 0 %.

**Reward penalty (C2).** The penalty weight λ moves the policy along a
trade-off between force and task completion, but no weight achieves both.
With λ ≤ 0.05 the penalty is too weak and the policy behaves like C1 (peak
force 12.3–13.6 N, 0–1 % success). With λ = 5 it is so strong that the policy
never completes the hold (0 %). Only λ = 0.5 produces safe feeding, 52.3 ±
45.5 % success, and it is strongly seed-dependent (0 / 74 / 83 %): one seed
reached the feeding pose in only 4 % of the episodes and never completed the
hold. Tuning the penalty therefore both requires a sweep
and remains unreliable across training runs.

**Reactive shield (C3).** Retracting once the force exceeds the trigger level
raises success to 74.0 ± 37.3 % (31 / 94 / 97 %), with 1.05 % of steps above
F_d. Because the shield acts only after the threshold has been crossed, it
cannot prevent the first overshoot, and each retraction interrupts the hold:
C3 has the largest force impulse of all conditions (9.2 ± 8.2 N·s). Its
policies rarely trigger the shield (intervention rate 3.3 %); the abrupt
retraction appears to teach them to stay below the trigger on their own,
which works for two seeds but not for the third.

**Predictive shield (C4).** C4 succeeded in all 300 evaluation episodes
(100 / 100 / 100 %), never exceeded F_d, and kept the peak force at
9.17 ± 0.24 N, just below the threshold. It also has the lowest impulse of the
methods that succeed (4.18 ± 0.08 N·s, 45 % of C3's) and a time to success
comparable to C3 (0.95 ± 0.02 s vs 1.05 ± 0.40 s). In the success–force plane
(Fig. X), C4 lies outside the curve traced by the penalty weights: no value of
λ reaches its success rate at a force below the danger threshold.

C4 modified the action in 90.3 ± 1.8 % of the control steps. The learned
policy thus leaves the deceleration near the face to the shield rather than
internalising the constraint, in contrast to C3.

## Design of the predictive shield

Table 2 reports C3 and C4 under three versions of the shields, each trained
and evaluated with the same protocol.

**Retreat direction.** In the first version, both shields measured the margin
as the straight-line distance from the spoon tip to the mouth point and
retreated along that line. One C4 seed failed (7 % success, 19.5 % of steps
above F_d): its policy held the bowl against the upper lip, where "away from
the mouth point" points forward *and upward*, into the lip. The lip deflected
the arm and the bowl slid 1–2 mm deeper per step. Freezing the arm stopped
this motion, and a full retreat pulled the spoon out at once, so the spoon was
not trapped; the one-step kinematic prediction simply cannot anticipate motion
deflected by contact. Measuring the margin as the horizontal depth in front of
the mouth and retreating straight out along the face axis — as a human feeder
withdraws a spoon without spilling — removed this failure (84 % for that seed).
It left another seed parked exactly on the limit, where overshoots of at most
0.3 N reduced its success to 51 %.

**Robust margin.** We measured C4's one-step prediction error in contact over
639 steps within 3 cm of the mouth: the tip moved at most 0.71 mm deeper than
predicted (99th percentile 0.49 mm), i.e. 0.35 N at the contact stiffness of
500 N/m. Both shields were therefore given a margin of twice this value,
0.7 N: C4 keeps the predicted force below 9.3 N and C3 triggers at 9.3 N. The
margin raised C4 to 100 % in all seeds, while C3 did not improve on average
(74.0 % vs 77.3 % without the margin): a lower trigger does not change the fact
that a reactive shield acts after the overshoot. The margin is thus not a
tuning knob that favours C4 alone; it absorbs a measured model error that
only a predictive shield can act on in time.

## Limitations

- **Contact model.** Contact force is computed from a penalty model — a
  virtual sphere of radius 4 cm around the mouth point with k = 500 N/m — not
  from the physical contact between spoon and face. The face colliders stop
  the spoon, but the forces they exert (e.g. on the upper lip) are not part of
  the metrics.
- **Statistical power.** Three seeds and 4 × 10^5 steps per run; the penalty
  sweep has four weights, with only one in the informative range. The
  per-seed variance of C2 and C3 is large.
- **Margin.** The margin was derived from policies trained without it; a
  sensitivity analysis over its value is left to future work.
- **Scene.** The face is rigid, no food is simulated, and the episode ends
  after the hold, without the withdrawal of the spoon.
